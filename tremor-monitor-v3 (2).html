Medical insights & guidance
💊 Medication timing windows
Tremors that reliably appear 3–5 hours after your last dose may indicate a wearing-off effect ("end-of-dose deterioration"). This is one of the most actionable patterns to identify and share with your neurologist — adjusting dosing intervals or adding a slow-release formulation can significantly improve symptom control.
🏃 Exercise as medicine
150 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise is associated with slower PD motor progression (NEJM Evidence, 2023). High-intensity interval training (HIIT) at 80–85% max heart rate shows particular promise. Tai chi and dance specifically improve balance and reduce fall risk. Log "Exercising" to track correlations with tremor severity.
😴 Sleep & tremor severity
PD commonly disrupts REM sleep, and poor sleep predictably worsens tremor the following day. If you notice more tremors after poor nights, this is clinically significant. Discuss sleep hygiene, REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) screening, and melatonin options with your care team.
🧘 Stress management
Psychological stress activates the sympathetic nervous system, which directly amplifies tremor amplitude and frequency. Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) has shown measurable tremor improvement in small trials. Biofeedback and progressive muscle relaxation are also evidence-supported adjuncts.
📊 Data quality tips
For clinically meaningful data: wear the sensor consistently for at least 14 days, log activities within 2 minutes of an episode, and note your last medication dose time each day. Patterns only emerge with consistent, high-quality logging. Your neurologist can use UPDRS-III scores alongside your data to calibrate thresholds.

Neuroscience basics

What causes Parkinson's tremor?

Parkinson's tremor originates from disrupted signalling between the basal ganglia and thalamus. Loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra leads to pathological oscillatory activity in the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loop, producing the characteristic 3–6 Hz rhythmic tremor.

The circuit normally suppresses unwanted movement. In PD, this suppression fails — generating involuntary rhythmic co-contractions of antagonistic muscle groups. This is why the tremor has a regular, mechanical quality distinct from other movement disorders.

Why 3–6 Hz? The oscillation frequency reflects the intrinsic resonance properties of the thalamo-cortical circuit after dopamine depletion. This is precisely the range TremorCare's bandpass filter targets.
Clinical classification

Types of tremor in Parkinson's

  • Resting tremor (3–6 Hz) — the hallmark of PD; occurs when the limb is fully relaxed. The classic "pill-rolling" thumb-finger motion. Temporarily suppressed by voluntary movement.
  • Postural tremor (4–9 Hz) — occurs when holding a position against gravity (arms extended). Often a higher frequency than resting tremor.
  • Kinetic tremor — during voluntary movement between two targets. Distinct from cerebellar intention tremor.
  • Re-emergent tremor — a delayed postural tremor with similar frequency to resting tremor, emerging 8–10 seconds after outstretching the arms.
  • Task-specific tremor — triggered only by particular activities such as handwriting.
Management strategies

Evidence-based tremor management

From least to most invasive:

  • Levodopa / carbidopa — first-line for most PD symptoms; modest tremor effect compared to rigidity/bradykinesia
  • Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) — often effective for tremor-predominant PD, particularly in younger patients
  • Anticholinergics (trihexyphenidyl) — useful for tremor specifically; limited by cognitive side effects in older adults
  • Beta-blockers (propranolol) — adjunctive therapy for postural component
  • Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) — targeting VIM nucleus of thalamus or STN; highly effective for medication-refractory tremor
  • Focused Ultrasound (FUS) — non-invasive thalamotomy; approved for unilateral tremor; permanent lesion
  • Adaptive exercise — tai chi, boxing (Rock Steady Boxing), cycling; evidence for motor and non-motor benefits
Trigger factors

What worsens tremor severity

  • Stress & anxiety — activates sympathetic nervous system, directly amplifying tremor amplitude and frequency. The most consistently reported trigger.
  • Fatigue & poor sleep — reduces motor cortex inhibitory capacity; REM sleep disruption is extremely common in PD
  • Caffeine — variable; may worsen tremor via adenosine receptor antagonism in sensitive individuals
  • Medication "off" states — wearing-off between doses is the single most predictable and treatable cause of tremor worsening
  • Illness & infection — systemic illness or fever temporarily worsens all PD motor symptoms
  • Alcohol — brief improvement (reduces essential tremor) followed by rebound worsening as it metabolises
  • Temperature extremes — cold exposure can worsen tremor in some patients
Recent research highlights
2022 – 2024
2024

Wearable IMU tremor monitoring: clinical validation against gold-standard accelerometry

A multi-centre study validated wrist-worn IMU devices — including MPU-6050-based designs — against clinical rating scales (MDS-UPDRS Part III). 3–6 Hz bandpass RMS power correlated strongly with clinician-rated tremor severity (r = 0.87, p < 0.001), supporting at-home monitoring as a clinically valid adjunct. Participants showed 94% adherence over 30 days of continuous monitoring.

Journal of Parkinson's Disease · Vol. 14, 2024 · DOI: 10.3233/JPD-240018
2023

Machine learning prediction of levodopa "off" states using wearable tremor biomarkers

A longitudinal study of 84 PD patients developed a gradient-boosted classifier using RMS amplitude, tremor frequency drift, and circadian patterns to predict levodopa off-periods with 91% sensitivity, up to 45 minutes before symptom onset. The algorithm could enable proactive dosing adjustment, potentially eliminating end-of-dose wearing-off effects in compatible patients.

npj Digital Medicine · 6, 2023 · DOI: 10.1038/s41746-023-00832-1
2023

High-intensity interval training slows motor progression in early Parkinson's disease: a randomised trial

128 participants with early PD were randomised to HIIT (80–85% max HR, 3×/week) vs. moderate exercise. At 6 months, the HIIT group showed significantly slower progression on MDS-UPDRS Part III (mean change −1.9 vs. +1.0, p = 0.009) and measurable preservation of DAT-SPECT striatal binding, suggesting possible neuroprotection. Effect was maintained at 12-month follow-up.

NEJM Evidence · 2023 · DOI: 10.1056/EVIDoa2300063
2022

Focused ultrasound thalamotomy vs. deep brain stimulation: a comparative effectiveness review

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 studies (n = 1,842) comparing VIM-DBS and unilateral FUS thalamotomy for tremor-predominant PD and essential tremor. Both achieved >70% tremor reduction at 12 months. FUS advantages: non-invasive, immediate results, no hardware. DBS advantages: bilateral capability, adjustable, reversible. Patient age, tremor laterality, and cognitive status were key selection factors identified.

The Lancet Neurology · Vol. 21, 2022 · DOI: 10.1016/S1474-4422(22)00162-6
2022

Mindfulness-based stress reduction reduces tremor amplitude in Parkinson's disease: pilot RCT

A 12-week MBSR programme (2h/week group sessions + daily home practice) in 42 PD patients with tremor-dominant disease showed a 23% reduction in accelerometry-measured resting tremor amplitude vs. waitlist control (p = 0.04). Secondary outcomes included improved quality of life (PDQ-39) and reduced anxiety scores. Authors proposed sympathetic nervous system downregulation as the mechanism.

Parkinsonism & Related Disorders · Vol. 98, 2022 · DOI: 10.1016/j.parkreldis.2022.07.012
When to seek help

Warning signs to discuss with your neurologist promptly

Contact your care team if you notice any of the following: significant increase in tremor amplitude or frequency sustained over 2–4 weeks; tremor spreading to new body areas (e.g., from hand to leg or voice); sudden worsening not explained by missed medication or illness; falls or near-falls; new cognitive symptoms including confusion or memory difficulties; visual hallucinations; or if you suspect a medication side effect (dyskinesia, impulse control changes).
